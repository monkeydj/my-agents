## 1. Eye-tracking research on reading patterns (F-pattern, Z-pattern, Nielsen Norman Group studies)

**F-shaped pattern (original 2006 study).** NN/G's eye-tracking study fitted 232 users with eye-tracking cameras and recorded how they looked at thousands of web pages. The dominant scan path: a horizontal sweep across the top of the content, a second (shorter) horizontal sweep somewhat lower, then a vertical scan down the left side of the page — forming an "F" shape. [F-Shaped Pattern For Reading Web Content (Original Study)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/). Practical implication: content and keywords placed at the start of headlines, paragraphs, and bullet points get disproportionately more eye fixations than content buried mid-paragraph or on the right side.

**2017 update — pattern persists but isn't universal.** NN/G revisited the research nearly a decade later and confirmed the F-pattern is "alive and well" on both desktop and mobile, but clarified it is one of several patterns, not a fixed law — layered/structured pages produce more varied scan paths than the F-shape (see below). [F-Shaped Pattern of Reading: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/).

**Why it matters for formatting rules:** the F-pattern is a *symptom* of poor scannability, not a desirable target — NN/G explicitly frames it as evidence users are hunting for signal in unstructured text and giving up after the first two lines. Good formatting (front-loaded headers, short first sentences, left-aligned key terms) is what breaks the F-pattern into fuller, more complete reading — meaning a strong F-pattern in analytics is a warning sign about the content's structure, not a pattern to design toward.

**Layer-cake pattern — the better alternative.** NN/G identifies a second, more effective scan pattern: users fixate mostly on headings and subheadings, with occasional deliberate dips into body text, producing a heatmap that looks like horizontal stripes separated by blank space (cake / frosting / cake). [The Layer-Cake Pattern of Scanning Content on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/); [Text Scanning Patterns: Eyetracking Evidence](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/). NN/G states this is "by far the most effective way" users scan pages short of reading every word — but it only emerges when headings/subheadings "visually stand out on the page and are descriptive." Vague or decorative headers collapse the pattern back into F-shaped skimming. **Direct rule implication:** heading quality (specific, front-loaded, visually distinct) is the single biggest lever for which scan pattern a reader falls into.

**Z-pattern.** A simpler, top-left → top-right → diagonal → bottom-right scan path that occurs on sparse layouts (landing pages, ads) engineered to funnel attention toward one call to action, rather than pages with substantial text content. [From F to Z: How Users Read Your Content](https://www.acquia.com/blog/content-reading-patterns). It is largely irrelevant to dense prose/documentation formatting — it's a low-content-density pattern, not a reading-comprehension one.

## 2. Headers/subheadings — effect on scannability and information retrieval

**General finding.** There is substantial empirical evidence that headings influence both comprehension during reading and retrieval after reading: they improve memory for text organization, activate readers' prior knowledge about the topic, and can bias what gets emphasized in comprehension. [Effects of Headings on Text Processing Strategies — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414729/).

**Retrieval/search benefit is the strongest, most consistent effect.** Locating information in a document is measurably easier when readers can use headings/subheadings as organizers — readers who correctly answered "major idea" questions were reliably better able to use the text's heading structure to relocate where the answer appeared than readers given the same text without headings. [How do headings affect text-processing? — Reading Research and Instruction](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19388079109558067). This is a retrieval effect specifically — headings act as a navigable index, not just decoration.

**Effect on raw comprehension is more nuanced than the retrieval effect.** Some studies find headings bias *what* is remembered (topics matching the heading's emphasis) rather than uniformly improving depth of comprehension — i.e., headings shape attention allocation, which can help (signals what matters) or selectively distort recall toward heading-flagged content at the expense of unflagged content. [How the relationship between text and headings influences readers' memory](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247328799_How_the_relationship_between_text_and_headings_influences_readers'_memory).

**Rule implication:** headers are best justified as retrieval/navigation aids (skimming to find a specific answer) rather than as a guaranteed comprehension boost for linear, first-pass reading — and a heading's wording sets what the reader will remember, so an inaccurate or vague heading actively misdirects recall rather than being a neutral label.

## 3. Bullets/lists vs. prose — when lists aid comprehension vs. fragment it

**When lists help.** Structured/bulleted formats can outperform paragraphs for retention and comprehension *speed*: reported figure is users consuming information roughly 25% faster in bullet form vs. standard prose. [The psychology of scannable content and bullet points — Yoast](https://yoast.com/the-psychology-of-scannable-content-and-bullet-points/) — note this is an industry/SEO source, not a peer-reviewed study; treat the 25% figure as directional, not a validated statistic, and flag it as such in any downstream rule. The mechanism given is that each bullet becomes a discrete unit for working memory, reducing per-item processing load; this is tied to Miller's "7±2" working-memory capacity — lists that respect a 5-9 item ceiling are chunked in line with working-memory limits, lists beyond that overload it. [Bullet Lists Vs Paragraphs: What LLMs Prefer](https://www.geekytech.co.uk/bullet-lists-vs-paragraphs-what-llms-prefer/).

**When lists hurt — the connective-tissue problem.** Bullets strip out connective words ("but," "and," "so," "because") that in prose encode the *relationship* between ideas (causal, contrastive, sequential). Without those connectives, a list of bullets can't unambiguously say how its items relate — a list of causally-linked facts rendered as parallel bullets reads as if the items were independent, when they are not. [Why bullets won't make your case — Emphasis](https://www.writing-skills.com/bullets-wont-make-case). This is the direct mechanism for "fragmenting" comprehension: lists are structurally suited to parallel/independent items (features, steps, discrete facts) and structurally poor for argumentative or causal content, where the connective logic *is* the content.

**Rule implication (ties to prima-flint's own register-ladder logic already in this repo):** the deciding test is not "is this a list of things" but "do these items relate causally, or are they atomic and parallel?" Causal/argumentative content should stay in sentences even if it looks listable; only genuinely parallel, non-sequential items should convert to bullets. Numbered lists are the sub-case for genuinely sequential/chronological steps.

**Peer-reviewed evidence — effect is real but conditional, not universal.** A study on bulleted lists and enumerations in formatted paragraphs ran three experiments and found bulleted-list format had a *positive* effect on recall of the listed items themselves — but this benefit was reduced when the listed components were heterogeneous (i.e., not genuinely parallel in kind), and two further experiments found bulleting had a *negative* effect on recall of the surrounding (non-listed) text. [How bulleted lists and enumerations in formatted paragraphs affect recall and evaluation of functional text — John Benjamins](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/idj.21.2.06jan). This is direct evidence for the "fragmentation" cost named in this section's title: pulling items into a bulleted list can help recall of *those items* while actively hurting recall of the prose around them — meaning over-bulleting a document doesn't just look worse, it measurably degrades memory for whatever is left in paragraph form nearby.

## 4. Whitespace and visual chunking — effect on perceived difficulty and actual comprehension

**Perceived difficulty.** Unbroken "wall of text" formatting makes content *look* harder before a reader processes a single word — chunked content avoids this by visually signaling flow and organization, which lowers the reader's up-front difficulty estimate independent of the actual content. [What Research Tells Us About Chunking Content — eLearning Industry](https://elearningindustry.com/chunking-content-what-research-tells-us). This is a perception effect, separate from comprehension itself: two identical texts can be judged differently hard purely on layout.

**Actual comprehension — margin whitespace.** Reported finding: increased white space at page margins improved reading comprehension by roughly 20%. [The Value of White Space — Hurley Write](https://www.hurleywrite.com/the-value-of-white-space/) (treat as a secondary/industry citation of underlying research rather than a primary study; the direction and rough magnitude are consistent with the chunking literature but the original source study isn't independently confirmed here).

**Mechanism — working memory limits.** Working memory has a hard capacity limit, and once content exceeds that limit before it can be chunked into a unit, comprehension and learning both degrade sharply. Chunking (via whitespace, paragraph breaks, and grouping of "related, logical, meaningful, and sequential segments") reduces the load per unit the reader must hold at once, which is the proposed mechanism for the comprehension gains, not just the perception gains. [What Research Tells Us About Chunking Content — eLearning Industry](https://elearningindustry.com/chunking-content-what-research-tells-us). NN/G's own guidance converges on the same operational rule: chunk by writing shorter paragraphs separated by white space, with each paragraph carrying one coherent unit of meaning.

**Reading-behavior tie-in.** Chunking also supports skimming, which usability research finds is the dominant reading mode for adults online — meaning whitespace/chunking serves both the skimmer (faster location of relevant chunks) and the close reader (lower working-memory load per chunk) simultaneously, rather than trading one off against the other.

**Neuroscience caveat (limited direct applicability).** A two-stage chunking study using EEG found readers detect familiar lexical chunks within ~100ms and then process larger ("global") chunks with priority over smaller ("local") ones, beginning ~160ms vs. ~220ms respectively — evidence that hierarchical chunk-level processing, not character-by-character reading, is the brain's default strategy. [How Do We Segment Text? Two-Stage Chunking Operation in Reading — eNeuro/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7294464/). Caveat: this study used Chinese four-character strings, a language without explicit word-boundary spacing, so its numeric timings don't transfer directly to English prose formatting — but the qualitative principle (readers default to hierarchical chunk-level, not linear, processing) is consistent with and supports the whitespace/paragraph-chunking guidance above.

## 5. Tables — when tabular structure aids vs. hinders (contrast with prose)

**Strong RCT evidence for tables over prose — but only for a specific content shape (comparative/numerical risk data).** A registered-report randomized trial on "fact boxes" (simple tabular risk-communication messages) tested over 2,300 participants across multiple medical topics, comparing tabular vs. prose presentation of the same information. Fact boxes produced measurably better comprehension immediately and slightly better knowledge recall six weeks later, compared to the same content presented as text — and this pattern held consistently across both medical topics tested and across all levels of participant numeracy and education. [Risk communication in tables versus text: a registered report randomized trial on 'fact boxes' — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7137953/). This is one of the strongest primary-study data points in this whole research set: a pre-registered RCT, not an industry blog claim, with a durability check (6-week follow-up) rather than just immediate recall.

**Why tables win for this content shape.** Tables make data easy to scan and compare side-by-side; the same numbers in prose force the reader to hold values in working memory across sentences to compare them, which is exactly the working-memory bottleneck problem chunking (section 4) exists to avoid. Text, in contrast, is better suited to narrative explanation, causal reasoning, and context — the same "connective tissue" argument made in section 3 for prose vs. bullets applies here: tables strip relational/causal language the same way bullets do.

**Cost of tables — split-attention.** Combining tables with surrounding prose analysis carries a real cognitive cost: readers must continuously move between dense paragraphs and the table, manually aligning the narrative claims with the specific cells they reference — a well-documented "split-attention effect" in multimedia/document design. This means a table is not free scaffolding; every table added is also a navigation tax on the reader, paid every time the surrounding prose refers back to it. [Better Think with Tables: Tabular Structures Enhance LLM Comprehension for Data-Analytics Requests](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.17189v2) discusses the general table/analysis interplay pattern.

**Rule implication:** the deciding test for tables mirrors section 3's bullets test — is the content fundamentally comparative/numerical (multiple items, each scored across the same set of attributes)? If yes, a table beats prose, per the RCT evidence above. If the content is explanatory, causal, or narrative, prose beats a table, and forcing it into a table strips the connective logic the same way over-bulleting does. A table immediately followed by prose that merely restates its cells in sentence form is pure redundancy — pick one.

## 6. Bold/emphasis — effective use vs. overuse (banner blindness, emphasis fatigue)

**Core mechanism: emphasis is relative, not absolute.** Salience only works via contrast with surroundings — the same HCI research area that studies banner blindness notes visual attention theory holds that something reads as salient only when it differs from what's around it. [How Humans Read Webpages: Reducing Banner Blindness](https://www.adpushup.com/blog/banner-blindness/). Direct consequence for text: bolding one word in a sentence full of plain text draws the eye; bolding half the words in every paragraph removes the contrast that made bolding work in the first place — "using bold formatting excessively essentially emphasizes nothing, since everything is emphasized," and overuse is reported to cause eye fatigue and reduced comprehension.

**Banner blindness as the visual-search analogue.** Named, peer-reviewed research on banner ads ("High-cost banner blindness: Ads increase perceived workload, hinder visual search, and are forgotten") found that the presence of banner-style visual elements increased participants' perceived mental workload, degraded their visual search performance, and left the bannered content itself less likely to be remembered afterward — the visual-salience trick backfires on both the task at hand and on recall of the emphasized element itself. [High-cost banner blindness — ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228340449_High-cost_banner_blindness_Ads_increase_perceived_workload_hinder_visual_search_and_are_forgotten). A related eye-tracking study asked directly whether banner blindness is genuine (i.e., do users' eyes actually avoid banner-like regions) and found supporting eye-tracking evidence for the effect. [Is Banner Blindness Genuine? Eye Tracking Internet Text Advertising — ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229884387_Is_Banner_Blindness_Genuine_Eye_Tracking_Internet_Text_Advertising). The mechanism generalizes beyond ads: readers learn to tune out any recurring visual pattern that reliably fails to carry new information, whether that pattern is an ad banner or an over-used bold/highlight convention.

**Root cause: information overload / information fatigue.** Underlying driver for banner blindness (and by extension emphasis fatigue) is information overload — once scanning becomes the reader's dominant mode (established in section 1's F-pattern/layer-cake findings), any element that doesn't reliably signal "this is worth stopping for" gets filtered out by the reader's attention system, and once filtered, it stays filtered for that document (habituation) even where genuinely important. [Banner Blindness — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banner_blindness).

**Rule implication.** Bold/emphasis should be reserved for the minority of spans in a document that are genuinely more important than their surrounding text (a real risk, a required action, a number the reader must not miss) — this matches the existing repo rule's own "Emphasis Escalation" guidance (security warnings, irreversible-action confirmations). Applying bold to routine facts, topic sentences, or "just to help it stand out" spends down the contrast budget the document has, and each additional bold span makes every other bold span in the same document less effective — the cost compounds, it doesn't stay flat.
