## 1. Cognitive Load Theory basics (Sweller) — intrinsic/extraneous/germane load, applied to written text

Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), originated by John Sweller, models working memory as a limited-capacity system that processes three distinct kinds of mental load during any learning or comprehension task. [Cognitive Load Theory — Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/cognitive-load-theory)

- **Intrinsic load** — the load inherent to the material itself: how complex the ideas are, how many interacting elements the reader must hold in mind at once ("element interactivity"), and how much prior knowledge/expertise the reader brings. Applied to text: a sentence introducing five new interdependent concepts at once has high intrinsic load regardless of how well it's written; a reader already expert in the domain experiences lower intrinsic load for the same sentence. [Cognitive Load Theory — Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/cognitive-load-theory)
- **Extraneous load** — load caused by *how* the material is presented, not by the material's inherent difficulty. This is unnecessary mental effort from poor formatting, ambiguous phrasing, buried structure, or irrelevant content. Applied to text: a long unbroken paragraph, a sentence with nested clauses that force the reader to re-parse, or jargon left unglossed — all add extraneous load without adding any understanding. This is the category formatting rules can most directly reduce. [Cognitive Load Theory — Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/cognitive-load-theory)
- **Germane load** — the productive mental effort spent building and automating schemas (mental models) in long-term memory — this is "good" load, the actual work of comprehension/learning. Applied to text: effort the reader spends connecting a new fact to what they already know, or following a logical throughline, is germane and worth preserving even if it "costs" some cognitive effort.

**Gibson's Dependency Locality Theory (syntactic working memory)**: separately from Sweller's instructional-design framework, psycholinguist Edward Gibson modeled sentence processing itself as a working-memory-limited task. His theory holds that memory and expectation are the two main costs in parsing a sentence: **storage cost** (the number of predicted grammatical structures the reader must hold open — e.g., an incomplete clause waiting for its verb) and **integration cost** (the distance, in intervening words, between a word and the earlier word it depends on). [Linguistic complexity: locality of syntactic dependencies — Gibson, MIT](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf)

- Gibson's model puts a hard ceiling on syntactic working memory: roughly **four** simultaneous unresolved local dependencies before a sentence becomes effectively unprocessable — center-embedded clauses stacked beyond that point (e.g., "the rat the cat the dog chased killed died") become unreadable regardless of vocabulary difficulty. [Linguistic complexity: locality of syntactic dependencies — Gibson, MIT](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf) Notably this ceiling of ~4 lines up with Cowan's independently-derived ~4-chunk working-memory limit (Section 2) — two separate research traditions (sentence parsing vs. general working memory) converge on the same rough capacity number.
- This explains why **subject-extracted relative clauses** ("the reporter who attacked the senator admitted the error") are easier to process than **object-extracted relative clauses** ("the reporter who the senator attacked admitted the error") — the latter requires holding "the reporter" in memory longer before its grammatical role resolves, i.e., higher integration cost. [Linguistic complexity: locality of syntactic dependencies — Gibson, MIT](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf)
- **Garden-path sentences** (sentences that initially mislead the parser into the wrong structure, e.g., "the horse raced past the barn fell") are hard to process not because of vocabulary but because the reader must discard an in-progress mental structure and rebuild it — a direct, sentence-level example of extraneous load caused purely by structural ambiguity, not informational content. [Locality and Parsing Complexity — Journal of Psycholinguistic Research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1026528912821)
- **Practical implication for formatting rules**: avoid center-embedding (a clause nested inside a clause nested inside another clause) and avoid long-distance dependencies (subject and verb separated by many intervening words) — these tax syntactic working memory independent of a sentence's raw word count, so a word-count-only rule can miss this failure mode. Prefer sentence structures where the reader can resolve each grammatical dependency quickly rather than holding several open at once.

**Key implication for writing rules**: the goal is not to minimize all cognitive load — it's to minimize *extraneous* load (formatting/clarity failures) so that the reader's limited working-memory budget goes toward *germane* load (actually understanding the content) rather than being consumed by *intrinsic* load the topic can't avoid. A formatting rule that strips out connective words or context to "simplify" can backfire if it increases extraneous load (forcing the reader to reconstruct relationships the writer removed) even while shortening the surface text. [Cognitive Load Theory — ScienceDirect Topics overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/psychology/cognitive-load-theory)

## 2. Working memory limits and chunking — why short sentences/paragraphs reduce load

**Miller's "magic number seven" (1956)**: George Miller's classic finding is that people can hold roughly 7±2 discrete items in short-term memory at once — but Miller himself framed "seven" as a rough, largely rhetorical figure rather than a precise measured limit. [Miller's Law — Yukai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/millers-magical-number-seven-plus-minus-two-working-memory/)

**Cowan's revision to ~4 chunks (2001)**: Nelson Cowan's reanalysis of decades of working-memory studies found that when researchers control for the tricks people use to inflate capacity — silent rehearsal, grouping, and drawing on long-term knowledge — the real limit on pure working-memory capacity is closer to **four chunks**, not seven. Four is the capacity observed when neither rehearsal nor long-term memory can be used to combine items; seven only holds when chunking is unrestricted. [The Magical Number 4 in Short-Term Memory — Cowan, PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/COWTMN)

**Chunking is what matters, not raw item count**: memory capacity is measured in meaningful *chunks*, not raw data points. Expertise changes what counts as one chunk — e.g., chess masters hold 20+ individual pieces in memory using roughly 5 chunks because board patterns are pre-grouped by experience; with practiced recoding strategies, one experimental subject pushed digit span from 7 to 79 by inventing higher-level chunks. [Miller's Law — Yukai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/millers-magical-number-seven-plus-minus-two-working-memory/)

**Direct implication for text formatting**: a sentence or paragraph is only as easy to hold in working memory as the number of *chunks* it forces the reader to track simultaneously — not its word count per se. Short sentences and single-idea paragraphs reduce load because they cap the number of unresolved chunks the reader must carry before reaching a resolution point (e.g., the end of a clause or paragraph). A sentence that piles up 3-4 unrelated new facts before its main verb or conclusion risks exceeding the ~4-chunk working-memory ceiling identified by Cowan, forcing rereading. This gives a concrete mechanism for "one idea per sentence/paragraph" rules: they keep the in-flight chunk count under the empirically observed ceiling.

## 3. Sentence length & complexity research — readability formulas, what sentence length actually correlates with comprehension

**Flesch-Kincaid formulas**: developed from Rudolf Flesch's 1948 readability scale, which used average sentence length and average word length (syllables) as the two core inputs. Two variants exist:
- **Flesch Reading Ease (FRE)**: scores readability from sentence length and syllable count; higher scores = easier/more conversational text, lower = harder. [Flesch-Kincaid readability tests — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesch%E2%80%93Kincaid_readability_tests)
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL)**: converts the same inputs into a U.S. school-grade number, i.e., years of education nominally needed to understand the text. [Flesch-Kincaid readability tests — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesch%E2%80%93Kincaid_readability_tests)

Both formulas treat **more words per sentence** and **more syllables per word** as directly, monotonically lowering readability — this is a mechanical formula, not a direct comprehension study, but it operationalizes sentence length as a primary readability lever. [Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch Kincaid Grade Level — Readable](https://readable.com/readability/flesch-reading-ease-flesch-kincaid-grade-level/)

**American Press Institute sentence-length/comprehension study**: analysis of 410 newspapers correlated average sentence length with measured reader comprehension and found a near one-to-one relationship:
- Sentences of **8 words or fewer** → readers understood **~100%** of the content.
- Sentences of **14 words** → **~90%** comprehension.
- Sentences of **43 words or more** → comprehension fell to **under 10%**.
This is one of the most widely cited concrete thresholds tying sentence length directly to measured comprehension rates (not just a readability-formula score). [How long should a sentence be? — Wylie Communications](https://www.wyliecomm.com/how-long-should-a-sentence-be/); also cited in [Is there an ideal sentence length? — CL Comms](https://clcomms.com.au/writing/is-there-an-ideal-sentence-length/)

**Caveat / limitation**: Note this data is frequently repeated across secondary sources (marketing/comms blogs) without a directly locatable primary API study; treat the exact percentages as a widely-cited industry benchmark rather than a peer-reviewed, independently replicated finding. The directional claim (shorter sentences → higher comprehension) is well supported by readability-formula research broadly, but the precise "8 words = 100%" figure should be flagged as secondary-source-only.

**Exact formulas** (from the primary formula definitions): [Flesch-Kincaid readability tests — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesch%E2%80%93Kincaid_readability_tests)
- **Flesch Reading Ease**: `206.835 − 1.015 × (total words / total sentences) − 84.6 × (total syllables / total words)`. Higher = easier. A score of 90-100 is "very easy" (roughly 5th-grade level), 0-30 is "very confusing" (college-graduate level).
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level**: `0.39 × (total words / total sentences) + 11.8 × (total syllables / total words) − 15.59`. Output maps to a US school grade level.
- Note the FKGL formula weights sentence length (0.39) relative to syllables-per-word (11.8) differently than FRE — Reading Ease is more sensitive to polysyllabic words, while Grade Level gives comparatively more relative weight to sentence length. The formulas were developed/validated for U.S. Navy technical-manual readability in 1975.

**Other major readability formulas** (useful because they triangulate sentence length as a comprehension factor using independent methodologies — not all rely on syllable counting): [Readability formulas — Readable](https://readable.com/readability/readability-formulas/)
- **Gunning Fog Index**: `0.4 × [(words/sentences) + 100 × (complex words/words)]`, where "complex words" are those with 3+ syllables. Produces a grade-level estimate 0-20. Like Flesch-Kincaid, it directly weights average sentence length as a primary factor. [The Gunning Fog Index — Readable](https://readable.com/readability/gunning-fog-index/)
- **SMOG (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook)**: created by G. Harry McLaughlin; uses polysyllabic word counts. Considered a "gold standard" formula specifically in healthcare-material readability assessment. [How to Decide Which Readability Formula to Use — ReadabilityFormulas.com](https://readabilityformulas.com/how-to-decide-which-readability-formula-to-use/)
- **Coleman-Liau Index**: created by Meri Coleman and T.L. Liau; unlike the others, it avoids syllable counting entirely and instead uses **characters per word** (letter count) — originally designed because syllable-counting was computationally expensive. Widely used in medical, translation, and legal-document readability contexts, and tends to produce **lower** grade-level scores than Flesch-Kincaid, ARI, or SMOG on the same technical text. [How to Decide Which Readability Formula to Use — ReadabilityFormulas.com](https://readabilityformulas.com/how-to-decide-which-readability-formula-to-use/)
- **Dale-Chall Readability Formula**: unlike the syllable/letter-count formulas, this one scores based on **word familiarity** — the percentage of words in a text that fall outside a list of ~3,000 words familiar to average 4th-grade readers — reflecting that unfamiliar vocabulary, not just sentence length, drives comprehension difficulty. [How to Decide Which Readability Formula to Use — ReadabilityFormulas.com](https://readabilityformulas.com/how-to-decide-which-readability-formula-to-use/)
- **Convergence and limitation**: nearly every major formula treats sentence length as a primary readability input, which is strong indirect evidence that sentence length matters — but a 2025 arXiv analysis found that classic readability formulas (and even LLM-based estimators) are **poor predictors of actual reading ease** when validated against real reader outcomes, meaning these formulas should be treated as rough proxies, not precise comprehension measurements. [Readability Formulas, Systems and LLMs are Poor Predictors of Reading Ease — arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11150)

**20-word threshold for "long" sentences**: readability guidance commonly flags sentences over 20 words as measurably harder to process, used as a practical style-guide cutoff in plain-language and accessibility contexts. [Readability: Why Are Sentences Over 20 Words Considered Long? — Siteimprove](https://help.siteimprove.com/support/solutions/articles/80000447968-readability-why-are-long-sentences-over-20-words-)

## 4. Paragraph length and single-idea-per-paragraph findings

**Single-idea-per-paragraph as a structural test**: the recurring guidance across writing/editing sources is that a paragraph should hold exactly one main idea, and that idea should be summarizable in a single sentence — if the summary takes two sentences, the source material should have been two paragraphs. This "one-sentence summary test" is presented as more reliable than any word-count rule. [Ideal Paragraph Length — Word Count Checker](https://wordcountchecker.org/blog/ideal-paragraph-length/)

**Context-dependent length norms** (these vary by genre — no single universal number):
- **Academic/long-form writing**: traditional guideline of 5-8 sentences or ~100-200 words per paragraph, reflecting the need to fully develop an argument with evidence and analysis. [Ideal Paragraph Length — Word Count Checker](https://wordcountchecker.org/blog/ideal-paragraph-length/)
- **Web/digital content**: shorter norm of 2-4 sentences or ~40-70 words per paragraph, with a recommended line length of ~50-75 characters — shorter because online readers scan rather than read linearly. [Paragraph Length for Web Content — Letter Counter](https://lettercounter.org/blog/paragraph-length-web-content/)

**Paragraph breaks as cognitive rest points**: breaks are described functionally as places where working memory can consolidate what was just read before taking on new information — i.e., a paragraph break lets germane load convert into long-term schema before more extraneous/intrinsic load is added. [Paragraph Length and Readability Optimization — Jamil Hosain](https://www.jamilhosain.com/paragraph-length-and-readability-optimization/)

**Nielsen Norman Group scanning-behavior finding (verified against primary source)**: on the average web page, users have time to read at most **28%** of the words during an average visit, and **20%** is more typical/likely. [How Little Do Users Read? — NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) A related NN/g study found **79% of test users always scanned** any new page rather than reading word-by-word; only **16% read word-by-word**. [How Little Do Users Read? — NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) NN/g's 2006 eyetracking study additionally found users scan in an **F-pattern**, concentrating attention on words at the top and left of a block of text/page, and barely registering text elsewhere. [Eyetracking Study of Web Readers — NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/eyetracking-study-of-web-readers/)

This is a strong, well-sourced argument for front-loading the core idea in the first sentence of a paragraph (and the first words of a heading/bullet) — since scanning readers disproportionately weight paragraph-opening and left-aligned content, and most of the remaining sentence is likely to go unread.

**Topic-sentence position research**: across composition, linguistics, and psychology research, topic sentences are found to help readers remember text better and facilitate comprehension — particularly when the text is difficult or the reader lacks domain expertise (i.e., topic sentences matter most exactly when intrinsic load, Section 1, is already high). [Topic sentence — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_sentence)
- Empirical corpus analysis of IELTS Academic Reading texts found topic sentences positioned at the **start** of a paragraph 60% of the time, at the end 30% of the time, and in the middle only 10% of the time — confirming paragraph-initial placement as the dominant real-world pattern in text specifically selected/vetted for readability. [Positions, Frequency, and Types of Topic Sentences — ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371303733_Positions_Frequency_And_Types_Of_Topic_Sentences_Within_Paragraphs_In_IELTS_Academic_Reading_Texts)
- A 2025 study ("First Come, First Remembered: Text Position Influences Attention Allocation and Recall of Digital Texts," *Applied Cognitive Psychology*) found that **text position directly affects both attention allocation and recall** in digital reading — content placed earlier in a text receives more attention and is remembered better, a direct primacy effect applied to reading (not just listening/list-memory contexts). [First Come, First Remembered — Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.70144?af=R)
- Even **minor, unsignaled topic shifts between sentences** measurably hurt readability — meaning paragraph cohesion (staying on one topic within a paragraph, per Section 4's "single idea" rule) is not just an aesthetic preference but has a measured comprehension cost when violated. [Topic sentence — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_sentence)

**Sentence-length norm reaffirmed**: general writing (non-web-scanning context) is best served by sentences averaging 15-20 words, consistent with the American Press Institute comprehension curve in Section 3 (100% comprehension ≤8 words, ~90% at 14 words, <10% at 43+ words). [Sentence Length for Readability — Letter Counter](https://lettercounter.org/blog/sentence-length-readability/)

## 5. Plain Language research (plainlanguage.gov, ISO plain language standard) — concrete thresholds

(Note: plainlanguage.gov's `/guidelines/words/` page now redirects to `digital.gov/guides/plain-language` — the U.S. federal plain-language guidance moved hosts. The landing hub page has no numeric detail itself; it links out to "Writing for understanding" and "Principles of plain language" sub-guides.)

**Concrete numeric thresholds from U.S. federal plain-language guidance** (consistent across agencies that implement the plainlanguage.gov standard):
- **Sentence length**: average **15-20 words per sentence** (Office of Personnel Management guidance); CDC targets an average of **20 words per sentence**; CMS caps sentences at **20 words or fewer**. [search-aggregated federal guidance, verify against opm.gov/information-management/plain-language and cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/plain-language.html]
- **One idea per sentence**: sentences should be limited to a single idea — directly mirrors the CLT/chunking rationale in Sections 1-2 (avoid piling multiple chunks into one sentence).
- **Paragraph length**: CDC guidance limits paragraphs to **1 topic and up to 5 sentences**; other plain-language guides recommend an even tighter **2-3 sentences max** per paragraph for public-facing material.
- These numbers converge with the independently-sourced readability/comprehension figures in Sections 3-4 (15-20 word sentence average, single-idea paragraphs), suggesting plain-language practice and readability-formula research arrived at compatible thresholds independently.

**ISO 24495-1:2023 — "Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines"** (published June 2023): this is the first international standard for plain language. [ISO 24495-1:2023 — ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html); [ISO Plain Language Standard — PLAIN](https://plainlanguage.com/what-is-plain-language/iso-plain-language-standard/)

- **Four governing principles**: Relevance (audience immediately recognizes content is relevant to them), Findability (audience can easily locate the information they need), **Understandability** (audience understands the content on **first reading**), and Usability (audience can act on the information). [ISO Plain Language Standard — PLAIN](https://plainlanguage.com/what-is-plain-language/iso-plain-language-standard/)
- **Sentence-structure guidance (qualitative, not a fixed word-count)**: use active verbs over passive constructions, keep sentences short with clear structure, and avoid nested/embedded sentences (i.e., avoid stacking subordinate clauses that force the reader to hold an unresolved grammatical structure in working memory — directly tying back to the chunking/working-memory limits in Section 2). [ISO 24495: The New Plain Language Standard — Plain Numbers](https://plainnumbers.org.uk/news-insights/find-it-understand-it-use-it-the-iso-standard-redefines-plain-language-as-a-user-outcome/)
- **Important distinction from Flesch-Kincaid-style formulas**: ISO 24495-1 deliberately defines plain language as an **outcome** ("the audience understands on first reading") rather than a fixed numeric threshold (like "≤20 words/sentence"). This matters for a formatting-rules rewrite: a rule pinned to a single word-count number is a proxy for the real goal (first-read comprehension for the intended audience), and the ISO standard's framing suggests thresholds should be treated as calibratable heuristics, not hard universal limits — appropriate word/sentence-length ceilings can reasonably vary by audience expertise (ties back to "intrinsic load" varying with reader prior knowledge, Section 1).
- Applicability: the standard is designed to apply across technical writing, legislative drafting, and controlled-language contexts, and across written languages generally (though its worked examples are in English). [ISO 24495-1:2023 — ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html)

## 6. Cross-application: what this implies for technical/business writing specifically

**Technical documentation is an extraneous-load minimization problem**: applying Sweller's framework directly, technical writing's job is to minimize extraneous load (poor structure, inconsistent abstraction levels, unglossed jargon) so the reader's limited working memory budget goes toward intrinsic load (the actual technical complexity, which can't be removed) and germane load (building a working mental model of the system). [Cognitive Load Theory and Technical Writing — The Comprehension Engineer](https://medium.com/the-comprehension-engineer/cognitive-load-theory-and-technical-writing-a-foundation-for-better-documentation-2d805fbc41e3)

**Named extraneous-load sources specific to technical/API documentation**: a poorly designed API with inconsistent levels of abstraction is explicitly called out as a source of extraneous load — i.e., structural inconsistency in *how* information is organized (not just how it's worded) taxes working memory. [Cognitive Load: Definition, Examples & Best Practices — Docsie](https://www.docsie.io/blog/glossary/cognitive-load/)

**Concrete mitigation techniques with named mechanisms**:
- **Progressive disclosure** — reveal only what's needed for the current step, deferring detail until the reader needs it, so working memory isn't loaded with information that isn't yet actionable. [Docsie — Cognitive Load](https://www.docsie.io/blog/glossary/cognitive-load/)
- **Chunked information + clear visual hierarchy** — directly operationalizes the Miller/Cowan chunking research from Section 2 into document structure (headings, sections, bullet groups act as chunk boundaries).
- **Split-attention effect** — cognitive load rises when related information is split across multiple visual locations the reader must cross-reference (e.g., a code sample on one page, its explanation on another); the fix is to integrate related information into one visual unit. [Docsie — Cognitive Load](https://www.docsie.io/blog/glossary/cognitive-load/)
- **Modality effect** — presenting complementary information through different channels (e.g., a diagram alongside prose, rather than two blocks of prose) allows more parallel processing since visual and verbal channels have partially independent working-memory capacity. [Docsie — Cognitive Load](https://www.docsie.io/blog/glossary/cognitive-load/)
- **Plain language + step-by-step instructions with visual support + generous white space** — reduces the "poor design" species of extraneous load called out explicitly in the technical-writing literature. [Docsie — Cognitive Load](https://www.docsie.io/blog/glossary/cognitive-load/)

**Bulleted lists vs. prose — research is conditional, not universally pro-bullets**: this matters directly for a formatting-rules config, since "use bullets" is a common but oversimplified instruction.
- Research on functional/instructional text found **list-formatted instructions were better understood and recalled than the same content in paragraph form**, and list formatting reduced age-related and individual-difference gaps in both answer speed and accuracy — i.e., bullets particularly help less-skilled or more time-pressured readers. [How bulleted lists and enumerations in formatted paragraphs affect recall — John Benjamins](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/idj.21.2.06jan)
- The same body of research found the list-format benefit applies specifically to **recalling the content of homogeneous, parallel list items** (e.g., steps, features) — and that benefit **shrinks or reverses when items are heterogeneous** (not genuinely parallel in kind). [How bulleted lists and enumerations in formatted paragraphs affect recall — ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290470586_How_bulleted_lists_and_enumerations_in_formatted_paragraphs_affect_recall_and_evaluation_of_functional_ext)
- Critically, some studies found bulleting content **reduces recall of the surrounding prose** that isn't itself bulleted — meaning converting everything to bullets isn't a free win; it can come at the cost of the connective/explanatory text around the list. [How bulleted lists and enumerations in formatted paragraphs affect recall — John Benjamins](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/idj.21.2.06jan)
- **Content-type dependency**: bullets excel for factual information, feature lists, and step-by-step processes; they are measurably less effective for narrative content, complex arguments, and content where the relationships *between* items (not just the items themselves) carry the meaning. [Using Bulleted Lists in Academic Writing — Impactio](https://www.impactio.com/blog/using-bulleted-lists-in-your-academic-writing-to-improve-research-understanding)
- **Formatting-rule implication**: bullet only genuinely parallel, homogeneous items (steps, discrete facts, options) — an argument or chain of reasoning where each point depends on the previous one should stay in connected prose, since bulleting strips the causal/logical connective tissue that Section 1's "extraneous load" analysis identifies as necessary, not filler.

**Synthesis for a formatting-rules rewrite** (tying Sections 1-6 together):
1. **Cap chunk count per unit**: aim for sentences/paragraphs that introduce no more than ~4 new interdependent facts at once (Cowan's working-memory ceiling), not an arbitrary word count alone.
2. **Sentence length target ~15-20 words average**, hard-flag sentences over ~25-30 words for restructuring — convergent finding across Flesch-Kincaid weighting, the American Press Institute comprehension curve, and federal plain-language guidance (Sections 3, 5).
3. **One idea per paragraph, opening sentence carries the point** — because most readers (technical or business) scan rather than read linearly (NN/g: 20-28% of words read), and paragraph breaks function as consolidation checkpoints for working memory (Section 4).
4. **Preserve connective words/logical scaffolding** — cutting articles/conjunctions to "compress" text increases extraneous load by forcing readers to silently reconstruct the relationship between ideas; this is the CLT-grounded argument against over-aggressive shorthand compression (Section 1's key implication).
5. **Don't split related information across locations** (split-attention effect) — keep a code sample/diagram and its explanation together rather than forcing the reader to flip between sections.
6. **BLUF ("Bottom Line Up Front") for business writing**: leading with the main point/conclusion before supporting detail is a military-communication technique adopted broadly in business writing. It is explained by the **primacy effect** — a cognitive bias where information presented first is remembered more accurately and weighted more heavily — meaning readers retain and act on a conclusion better when it comes first rather than after buildup. [BLUF (communication) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication)); [Great communication: how to get your message across — Board Intelligence](https://www.boardintelligence.com/blog/great-communication-call-your-bluf) A related finding in presentation research: slide headlines that state the main point (an assertion) rather than a generic/ambiguous topic label measurably improve audience comprehension and learning of the material — the same "front-load the conclusion" logic applied to headings. [Great communication — Board Intelligence](https://www.boardintelligence.com/blog/great-communication-call-your-bluf) This directly reinforces the "lead with the conclusion" pattern and pairs with the NN/g scanning-behavior finding in Section 4 (readers disproportionately weight what's first).
7. **Calibrate thresholds to audience expertise, not a single universal number** — ISO 24495-1 defines plain language as an outcome (understand on first read) rather than a fixed metric, and intrinsic load is explicitly reader-expertise-dependent (Section 1); a formatting rule for an expert technical audience can tolerate somewhat denser sentences than one for a general business audience, provided first-read comprehension is preserved.
